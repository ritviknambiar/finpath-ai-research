Project overview

This independent research project explores how international students can evaluate the financial implications of studying in the United States. It combines economics, data analysis, artificial intelligence and behavioral psychology.

Objective

To give a student and their family a transparent way to see three things: the full four-year cost of a U.S. public college, not just the published price; how uncertain that cost is; and how personal priorities and fears can shape the decision. Every number comes from public data, the project's own code, or an assumption that is stated on this page.

Why it matters

Published prices leave out costs that a student will face, such as health insurance, flights home, visa fees and four years of price growth. International students are also generally charged out-of-state rates at public colleges. In this dataset, 95% of colleges charge out-of-state students more. At the typical college, out-of-state tuition is about 2.2 times in-state tuition (median $22,200 a year out-of-state vs $10,600 in-state).

What was built

An interactive web app with five parts:

  • Overview and Compare: the published price next to the fuller cost, for up to 10 colleges a student picks.
  • Simulation: 510 simulated futures per college, and the chance of going over a budget, in the student's home currency.
  • Best fit: a ranking that uses weights the student sets for affordability, location, prestige, family and friends, and safety.
  • Scenario: sliders for “what if” changes, and a link that shares the exact scenario.
  • AI Optimizer: a search for the most comfortable plan that fits a budget, with a plain-language explanation from an AI model.

Research questions

Each question states how far this project has answered it. Some parts are answered with data, some are modeled but not yet tested with real students, and one is future work.

Mostly addressed

1. How do tuition, living expenses and financial aid influence college affordability?

What the project does: it builds the four-year cost from tuition and fees, room and board, and books and other allowances (College Scorecard), plus assumed insurance, flights and visa fees, grown with inflation. It then shows how those parts add up and how many colleges fit a given budget (Figures 1 and 2).

Not covered: financial aid appears only as a scholarship amount a user can enter. The Scorecard's aid figures describe students who received U.S. federal aid, and international students are generally not in that group.

Partly addressed

2. How does financial uncertainty affect long-term educational investment?

What the project does: it measures the uncertainty in the cost: inflation, scholarship renewal, surprise costs and exchange rates. A simulation gives a range of outcomes and the chance of exceeding a budget (Figure 3).

Not covered: the project does not model the returns to education (earnings, employment) or how uncertainty changes a family's decision to invest. That part is future work.

Modeled, not yet tested with students

3. How do behavioral biases influence student financial decisions?

What the project does: it turns several biases into explicit, adjustable model parts: the gap between the published price and the full cost (anchoring on the sticker price), loss aversion (an overspend counts more than the same amount of savings), and preferences such as prestige, location and the influence of family and friends. See Finding 4.

Not covered: no survey or experiment with real students has been run yet. The model shows what these assumptions imply; it does not show how students actually behave.

Not yet addressed (future work)

4. How does international student spending contribute to local economies?

What the project has: a per-student breakdown of spending by category (Figure 1), which is one building block for this question.

What is missing: enrollment counts and an economic model of how spending ripples through a local economy. For context only, NAFSA and JB International estimate that international students contributed $42.9 billion to the U.S. economy and supported 355,736 jobs in 2024–25. This project did not reproduce or test that estimate.

Cost model

For each year, the model adds tuition and fees (minus any scholarship), housing, meals, books and the college's own other-expense allowance, health insurance, flights home and, in year one, visa fees. Housing and meals are split from the Scorecard's single room-and-board figure with a fixed 55/45 split, which is an assumption. Each cost grows at its own yearly rate from the table above.

Simulation of uncertainty

For each college, the model plays out the four years 510 times. Each run randomly varies the yearly growth of every cost category, the exchange rate (a random walk using the currency's 5-year trend and swing), whether a scholarship is renewed each year (85% chance a year; once lost, it stays lost), and surprise costs (25% chance a year, typically about $1,500 but sometimes much more). The random numbers are seeded, so results are repeatable. The output is a low case (10th percentile), a middle case (median), a high case (90th percentile), and the chance of exceeding a budget. The spreads used for the random variation are assumptions, not measurements.

Decision support

Two further tools use the same calculation. “Best fit” scores each college from 0 to 100 on affordability, location, prestige, family and friends, and safety, and combines the scores using weights the student sets. The “AI Optimizer” tests every combination of housing, bedroom and meal plan with the simulation and recommends the most comfortable one that stays within a budget and a chosen risk of overspending.

How AI is used

An AI model (Anthropic's Claude) is used in one place only: to explain, in plain language, results that the code has already calculated. It is never asked for tuition or any other price. The server builds the request itself from numbers and a few fixed words, and every number in the answer is checked against the app's own figures. An answer with a number that does not match is discarded and a built-in summary is shown instead. The tool works fully without the AI.

Results

All results are model estimates for the baseline student described above, in U.S. dollars, using the assumptions listed in the methodology. They are not predictions or quotes.

Key takeaways

  • At the median college, the modeled four-year cost is about 33% higher than the published price (for the middle 80% of colleges: 22% to 48% higher).
  • International students have far fewer affordable options: at $50,000 a year, 58% of the colleges fit, compared with 97% for an in-state resident.
  • In dollars alone, the uncertainty around the middle estimate is small: planning about 2% above it covers 90% of simulated futures. Exchange-rate changes are a separate risk, shown under Finding 3.

Finding 1: the modeled cost is about a third higher than the published price

For the median college, the modeled four-year cost is $192,000, against a published price of $144,000: 33% higher. Without inflation, it would be 26% higher.

Across the colleges, about 45% of the extra cost beyond the published price comes from the Scorecard's own books and other-expense allowances (real data), 33% from items assumed in the model (insurance, flights, visa fees), and 22% from four years of price growth. So roughly a third of the gap rests on assumed values that are the same for every college.

Where the money goes, on average: tuition and fees are 51% of the modeled total, housing and meals 29%, books and allowances 11%, and insurance, flights and visa fees 8%.

Finding 2: costs vary widely, and the international premium is large

The modeled four-year cost ranges from $115,000 to $394,000. The middle 80% of colleges fall between $149,000 and $275,000.

A student who is a resident of the college's own state would face a median modeled cost of $141,000, which is $51,000 less than the international student's median of $192,000. Figure 2 shows what that means for budgets.

Finding 3: in dollars, the uncertainty is small; the currency is a separate risk

Running the simulation for all 461 colleges, the likely range (the 10th to the 90th percentile) is about 3% wide, measured against the middle estimate. To be 90% sure of staying within a budget, a family would plan about 2% above the middle estimate (at most 3% for any college).

Figure 3 shows one typical college: The University of Texas at El Paso (TX), chosen because its modeled cost is closest to the median of all 461.

This narrow range is a result of the assumptions: small spreads in the growth rates, and no scholarship in the baseline. It does not include exchange-rate changes. Because every cost is paid in dollars, a family whose budget is in another currency carries that currency risk on the whole bill. As plain arithmetic, not a forecast:

Finding 4: behavioral biases are modeled, not yet measured

The project turns several ideas from behavioral economics into adjustable parts of the model:

  • Anchoring on the sticker price. The gap in Finding 1 is the size of the error a student would make by relying on the published price alone.
  • Loss aversion. In the “Best fit” score, going over budget counts a chosen number of times as much as having the same amount to spare. The default is 2, based on published estimates of about 2.25 (Tversky and Kahneman, 1992) and about 1.96 (a 2024 meta-analysis).
  • Preferences and influence. Prestige, location, the views of family and friends, and safety enter as weights that the student sets. A “worry about jobs and visas” setting makes affordability count more.

These are model parameters. No experiment or survey with students has been run yet, so this page makes no claim about how strongly any of them affects real decisions.

For context from existing research on U.S. households and students (not specifically international students): in a survey of U.S. household heads, 61% reported net public college costs that exceed the actual net costs, and 75% underestimated the earnings benefit of college (Bleemer and Zafar, 2015). Among college enrollees, the average belief of earning a bachelor's degree by age 30 was 92%, while about 70% actually did (Moschini, Raveendranathan and Xu, 2023). People can misjudge college costs and outcomes in both directions, which is why the project shows the full cost and its range.

Technologies used

  • Languages: JavaScript (ES modules), JSX, HTML, CSS. Command-line scripts run on Node.js.
  • Front end: React, Vite, Tailwind CSS (v4), Recharts for charts in the app, rc-slider for sliders.
  • Back end and AI: a Vercel serverless function (Node.js) that calls the Anthropic Claude API, with per-visitor rate limits.
  • Data and services: the College Scorecard (a CSV file) and the Frankfurter exchange-rate API.
  • Methods: a deterministic cost model, Monte Carlo simulation with a seeded random-number generator, percentile statistics, log-return statistics for exchange rates, weighted scoring, and an exhaustive search over plan options.
  • Tools: Visual Studio Code, Git and GitHub, Vercel (hosting the app), GitHub Pages (hosting this page).

AI assistance

Claude, an AI assistant from Anthropic, was used as a coding and writing assistant during development. Inside the finished app, Claude is used only for the plain-language explanations described in the methodology.

Limitations

Data

  • Only 461 public four-year colleges are included. Private colleges are not, so the results do not describe them.
  • The College Scorecard counts students who received U.S. federal aid. International students are generally not in that group, so its earnings and aid figures are only a rough guide for them. The project does not use them in its results.
  • Costs are for the 2025–26 academic year and are then grown with assumed rates. Real prices in the year a student enrolls will differ.
  • The exchange rates are reference rates, not what a bank or card charges.

Assumptions

  • Health insurance, flights and deposits are single assumed values used for every college, not data for each one.
  • The 55/45 split of room and board, the 25% discount for a shared bedroom, and the rule that a commute limit only decides whether off-campus housing is allowed have no data behind them.
  • The spreads of the random variation, the scholarship-renewal chance and the surprise-cost settings are assumptions.
  • The inflation rates come from published sources, but each is a single recent figure, not a forecast.

Method

  • The model has not been validated against real students' bills or surveys.
  • The behavioral parts are model settings. No human-subjects study has been done.
  • The project covers undergraduate costs only, and does not model earnings or employment after graduation.

Areas for future research

  1. Compare the model's estimates with actual student bills and a survey of international students.
  2. Test the behavioral ideas with a survey or experiment, for example by comparing students' own cost estimates with the modeled cost.
  3. Add the returns side (earnings and employment) to study long-term educational investment, taking care with data that exclude international students.
  4. Address the local-economy question with enrollment counts and an economic multiplier model.
  5. Replace assumed insurance, deposit and off-campus figures with data for each college, and add private colleges.
  6. Find data on aid to international students, such as institutions' Common Data Set reports.
